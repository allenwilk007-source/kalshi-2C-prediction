Here is the whole pipeline in six steps. Everything is in Python, and it all runs from one command that opens a local dashboard and refreshes the data in about three minutes. Step 1 rebuilds the HW5 spreadsheet exactly. Step 2 connects it to the real world: the four temperature datasets Kalshi settles on, and the year-to-year noise from El Niño and La Niña. Step 3 runs 100,000 versions of the future with different economic growth, energy use and climate sensitivity. Step 4 updates the starting point with 2026 data. Step 5 turns Kalshi’s price into a fair probability and checks 15 other Kalshi climate markets. Step 6 asks whether a student could have made money, hypothetically. At the end there’s an interactive Decision Lab where anyone can change these assumptions and make their own call. Why build it this way? Each step answers one doubt about the last. Does the Python model really match the homework? Step 1 checks every year. Does a smooth model say anything about one noisy year? Step 2 adds the noise. How sure can we be? Step 3. Does this year’s heat change things? Step 4. Is the market price really a probability? Step 5.
