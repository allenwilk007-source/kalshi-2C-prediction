HOW THE PRICE FORMS

From an order book to 70%

  • Yes pays $1 if 2°C happens; No pays $1 if not. So Yes + No = $1
  • Buyers post bids, sellers post asks; a trade happens when they meet
  • Best bid 68¢, best ask 72¢: the midpoint, 70¢, reads as 70%
  • Anyone who disagrees can trade, which pulls the price toward the crowd’s view

Order book on 18 Nov 2026. 21,757 contracts traded since July 2025; 4,142 still open.

Here’s exactly how Kalshi produces its number. There’s no model behind it, just an order book. Each contract is a simple bet. A Yes contract pays one dollar if any year before 2050 reaches 2°C on Kalshi’s rule, and nothing otherwise. A No contract pays one dollar in the opposite case. Since exactly one of them pays, a Yes and a No together are always worth one dollar, and that keeps the two prices consistent. On the right is the real order book from 18 November. The orange bars are people offering to sell Yes, the asks: 500 contracts at 72 cents, then more at 75, 76 and 84. The blue bars are people offering to buy Yes, the bids: only 20 contracts at 68 cents, then more down to 64. A trade only happens when a buyer accepts an ask or a seller accepts a bid. The best bid is 68 and the best ask is 72, so the midpoint, 70 cents, is the market’s price. A contract that pays one dollar with probability p is worth about p dollars, so 70 cents reads as a 70% chance. Why should we trust it at all? Because anyone who thinks 70% is wrong can trade against it and expect to profit, and that pressure pushes the price toward what the crowd as a whole believes.
