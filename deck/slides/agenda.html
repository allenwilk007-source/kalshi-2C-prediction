THE PLAN

Three questions, in order

PART 1

How does Kalshi get its number?

Traders buy and sell a $1 contract. The price they agree on is the crowd’s probability: 70%

PART 2

How do we get ours?

The class model, run thousands of times, gives a probability density. The area past 2°C is our number: 67–81%

PART 3

What could we do with them?

Turn the numbers into a hypothetical Yes, No or Pass, and see how the answer changes with what you believe

If you believe X will happen (emissions fall, the IPCC is right, 2049 won’t count), you can see the effect Y on the odds and on the payoff.
The talk has three parts, and each answers one question. Part 1: how does Kalshi get its number? We’ll look at what the contract pays, how the order book turns buyers and sellers into a price, and why that price is only roughly a probability once you count fees, spreads and how thin the market is. Part 2: how do we get ours? We start from the class model, add real data and year-to-year noise, run it thousands of times, and turn all those futures into a probability density function. The share of the density past 2.00°C is our probability. Depending on how much uncertainty we allow, that’s between 67% and 81%. Part 3: what could we do with the two numbers? We turn a forecast into a decision: buy Yes, buy No, or pass. Then we look at a few hypothetical strategies, and finally we personalise it: if you believe something specific will happen, for example that emissions fall to net zero, here’s what that does to the odds and to what you’d win or lose. Everything in part 3 is hypothetical; no trades were placed.
