READING THE PRICE

A price isn’t quite a probability

  • Fees and spread: 70% really means anything from 66% to 74%
  • Interest: Kalshi pays 3.5% a year on positions, so tying money up until 2050 barely matters
  • Thin market: one $1,344 order on 22 Sep 2026 moved the price about 10 points
  • Timing quirk: 2049’s data may come after the contract expires, worth ~5 points
Before comparing, we had to turn the price into a probability properly. Four things matter. Fees and the spread: buying Yes costs 72 cents plus a 1.4 cent fee, and selling gets 68 cents, so a fair belief could be anywhere from about 66% to 74%. Interest: a contract that pays in 2050 ties up money for over 20 years, which would normally drag the price down a lot. But Kalshi pays 3.5% interest on open positions, close to Treasury bill rates, so the drag is only about one point. Thin market: on 22 September 2026, one trader bought $1,344 of No in a single second, sweeping the order book from 76 cents down to 3 cents. The price has sat around 65 to 72 since. So the market’s probability rests on very little money. Timing quirk: the contract expires on 8 January 2050, but agencies usually publish each year’s figure in mid-January, so 2049 might not count. That’s worth about 5 points.
