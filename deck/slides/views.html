This slide puts the four views side by side on five dimensions, because a single probability hides most of what’s going on. Row one is the headline. Our 72-version model says 81%, the IPCC-calibrated version 67%, Kalshi 70%. The IPCC report itself doesn’t give this number at all, and row two explains why. Row two: what counts as 2°C. Kalshi asks about any single calendar year, confirmed by two of four datasets. We copied that rule exactly in both versions of our model. The IPCC defines crossing 2°C as a 20-year average, which smooths out El Niño years. A single hot year comes several years before the 20-year average gets there, so Kalshi’s question is easier to reach than the IPCC’s. Row three: timing. Both versions of our model put the first 2°C year at a median of 2044, with most futures between about 2038 and 2048. The IPCC’s middle scenario puts the 2041–2060 average at 2.0°C, which is the same story told with a 20-year average. Kalshi’s price doesn’t tell us when, only whether. Row four: the range. This is where the two versions of our model really differ. The highest reading before 2050 ranges from 1.89 to 2.43°C in the 72-version model but from 1.74 to 2.43°C in the IPCC-calibrated one. Same top, longer bottom tail, so fewer futures make it to 2°C. The IPCC’s own range for 2041–2060 is 1.6 to 2.5°C, close to our IPCC-calibrated version, which is why we built it. Row five: what each view can see. Our model sees CO₂ and random year-to-year noise. The market sees whatever traders know, including El Niño forecasts. The IPCC uses all greenhouse gases, aerosols and dozens of full climate models. That’s why we trust the IPCC’s width more than our own.
