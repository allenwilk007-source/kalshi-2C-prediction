  • Months the agencies haven’t published are filled from ERA5 daily data
  • The warm end of 2026 carries into 2027 (El Niño)
  • It barely moves the 2049 odds: only the long-run trend gets us to 2°C
The model’s starting point matters, so we updated it with 2026 data. NASA and NOAA have published through September and HadCRUT through July. For the missing months we used ERA5, Copernicus’s daily global temperature, through a regression linking ERA5 to each dataset. We back-tested this on 2023–2025 and widened the uncertainty until it was honest. 2026 comes out at about 1.52°C above pre-industrial, likely a record. October and early November were exceptionally warm, which looks like a developing El Niño. We found that warm late-year months carry into the next year, so 2027 should stay warm too. But here’s the surprise: it barely changes the 2050 question. A hot year fades within a few years. What decides the 2040s is the long-run trend, and that comes from emissions and climate sensitivity.
