1 · HOW WE GOT OUR NUMBERS

Thousands of futures, one probability density function

1 · Class model (HW2–HW5)
Kaya emissions → 5-box carbon cycle → CO₂ forcing → two-box temperature
2 · Real-world noise and Kalshi’s rule
El Niño-style swings each year; a year counts when the 2nd-highest of 4 datasets is ≥ 2.00°C
3 · Thousands of futures
Vary sensitivity, response speed, emissions and land use; start from 2026’s data
4 · Probability density function
Each future’s hottest reading, 2027–2049 → P(Yes) = area right of 2.00°C
This is how we got our numbers, in four steps. Step one is the climate model from our homework, exactly as in class. The Kaya identity turns population, GDP per person, energy per dollar and carbon per unit of energy into emissions. A five-box carbon cycle turns emissions into CO₂ concentration, a log formula turns that into radiative forcing, and a two-box model turns forcing into temperature. Our Python version matches the HW5 spreadsheet in every year. Step two makes it realistic. The class model draws one smooth line, but real years bounce around by about a tenth of a degree because of El Niño. We added that year-to-year noise, estimated from 1880–2025. We also copied Kalshi’s rule exactly: four agencies publish temperatures, and a year only counts when the second-highest of the four reads at least 2.00°C. Step three: one run gives one future, so we ran thousands. Each future uses different settings for climate sensitivity, how fast the climate responds, the emissions path and land-use emissions, and every one starts from where 2026 actually is. Step four is the probability density function. For every simulated future we record its hottest reading between 2027 and 2049. The chart is the density of those readings: where the futures land. A future pays Yes exactly when its hottest reading is at or past 2.00°C, so the probability of Yes is the area under the curve to the right of the gold line. Two curves, two numbers. The orange one averages 72 versions of the class model and puts 81% of its futures past 2°C. The blue one is the same model with its uncertainty widened to match the IPCC’s ranges: same middle, around 2.1°C, but wider, so only 67% lands past the line.
