2 · HOW KALSHI GETS ITS NUMBER

A price that traders agree on

  • A Yes contract pays $1 if any year before 2050 reaches 2°C (2 of 4 datasets)
  • Traders post offers to buy (bids) and sell (asks); a trade happens where they meet
  • Best bid 68¢, best ask 72¢: the midpoint, 70¢, reads as a 70% chance
  • After fees and the spread, 70% really means anywhere from 65% to 73%
  • A thin market: 4,142 contracts open, so one order can move the price
Now the market’s side. Kalshi doesn’t have a climate model. Its 70% is simply a price. Each contract pays one dollar if any year before 2050 reaches 2°C on the same rule we used, and nothing otherwise. A contract that pays a dollar with probability p is worth about p dollars, so its price in cents reads as a probability. The chart is the real order book from 18 November. The orange bars are traders offering to sell Yes, starting at 72 cents. The blue bars are traders offering to buy Yes, starting at 68 cents. A trade only happens when someone accepts one of those offers. The midpoint, 70 cents, is the market’s probability: 70%. Two cautions. First, trading isn’t free: buying Yes costs 72 cents plus a fee of about 1.4 cents, and selling gets only 68. So any belief between about 65% and 73% is consistent with this price. Second, the market is thin: about 4,100 contracts are open and only 20 contracts sit at the best bid. In September one trader’s order of about $1,300 moved the price by 10 points. So the market’s number rests on very little money.
